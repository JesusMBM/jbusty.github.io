How AI models respond to security decoys

Honeyquest tested which clues 21 AI models would investigate. They often selected planted decoys, while also selecting genuine weaknesses. Here is what those questionnaire choices mean for defenders.

By Jesus Bustillos-Molina · Reviewed September 4, 2026 · About 5 minutes

01 / AI security, explained

A test of what looks worth investigating

Cyber deception means planting convincing decoys, such as fake files or login details, to attract or detect an intruder. Honeyquest tests which clues look interesting without building a complete computer system. Participants see numbered lines from a file listing or web traffic record and mark what they would investigate, avoid as a trap, or leave alone.

Sources: Original human study

Researchers hold an answer key identifying ordinary items, genuine weaknesses and planted decoys. Participants do not see those labels before answering. One question can contain several targets, so someone can select both a decoy and a weakness.

Sources: LLM study, version 2

Horizon3.ai tested 21 models from 10 providers on 174 questions, repeated three times: 10,962 model responses. Each answer was separate, with no memory of earlier questions or live attack loop. Models returned structured choices and short explanations. The comparison used 3,669 earlier responses from 47 people with security-work or security-competition experience; they did not all answer every question.

Sources: LLM study, version 2 · Original human study

Choices on paper

The measured action was selecting a line to investigate. These figures do not report files opened, passwords stolen, systems compromised or successful attacks.

The decoy-selection rate was about 2.12 times the human rate, a difference of 41.5 percentage points. This is an aggregate over responses, not the percentage of models fooled. Model rates ranged from 61% to 92.5% in this setup. Models selected more items overall: the median number of investigate marks was three per question, compared with one for humans.

04 / AI security, explained

Attracting attention is different from diverting it

To test diversion, researchers compared paired questions: the same genuine weakness shown alone and alongside a decoy. In the human study, adding a decoy reduced selection of the weakness by 22% relative to the version without it. For models, the reported relative change was +9.9%.

Sources: LLM study, version 2 · Original human study

Relative change is not percentage points

For illustration, a 22% relative reduction from 50% would produce 39%: an 11-percentage-point drop. Those example rates are invented, not the study's starting and ending rates.

The model test specifically looked for a reduction; it did not establish an overall diversion benefit. It also does not establish that the apparent increase was a reliable harmful effect. One of five paired techniques did show a significant reduction for models: a fake developer access token in a web response header. The other four lacked statistically significant evidence of diversion in this test.

Sources: LLM study, version 2

05 / AI security, explained

What the August revision clarifies

Researchers also searched explanations for trap-related vocabulary and suspicion. Among responses to deceptive questions that triggered those rules, 73.4% still selected an intended decoy to investigate. In that same group, 20.1% marked the decoy as a trap to avoid and 6.5% left it unmarked.

Sources: LLM study, version 2

Version 2, revised August 17, separates the 73.4% result. The authors classified 48.5% of the suspicion-language group as correctly identifying a trap yet selecting it, and 24.8% as misidentifying it and selecting it. Published rounding makes these components sum to 73.3% rather than 73.4%.

Sources: LLM study, version 2

The classification uses text rules, including whether a negation appears within six words before matched trap vocabulary. It is not a direct measurement of understanding. The useful observation is narrower: a model's written warning can differ from its selected option.

Sources: LLM study, version 2

06 / AI security, explained

Measure what a defense changes

Honeyquest describes these models, questions and prompts. Repeated responses are not thousands of independent models, and a questionnaire cannot establish how a persistent agent with tools will behave during a real intrusion.

Sources: LLM study, version 2

For a practical test, ask whether a decoy creates a useful alert, delays progress, reduces access to genuine assets, or consumes time and computing budget. Those are proposed measures for live testing, not demonstrated Honeyquest outcomes. An appealing decoy becomes a useful defense only when the outcome helps the defender.

Words explained

LLM
Large language model: software trained on text that can generate answers and choose between described options.
AI agent
A system combining a model with tools so it can take actions through a task.
Decoy or honeyfile
A planted item designed to attract attention; a honeyfile is a file used as a decoy.
Credential
Information used to sign in, such as a password or access key.
Investigate mark
The questionnaire choice meaning ‘I would try this’; it does not perform an attack.
Trap mark
The original questionnaire choice identifying bait to avoid.
Diversion
Drawing attention away from a genuine weakness.
Statistically significant
Meeting a statistical test's threshold; this does not establish practical importance or guarantee the result generalizes.
