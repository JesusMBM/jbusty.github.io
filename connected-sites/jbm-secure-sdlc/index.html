01 / Software security

Four ideas with different jobs

A software development lifecycle describes how an idea becomes a product and how that product is maintained. Secure development adds decisions about what to protect, safeguards against misuse, checks that those safeguards work, and responses when problems appear. Threat modeling is a structured discussion of what could go wrong in a particular system.

Sources: NIST SSDF 1.1 · OWASP

How the approaches fit together
IdeaPurpose

STRIDEPrompt questions about six kinds of threat.

02 / Software security

Explore six development stages

These six stages are a teaching outline, not a mandatory NIST sequence. Teams revisit decisions and may work on several stages at once. Bringing security earlier is sometimes called “shifting left”; maintaining protection after release matters too.

Sources: NIST SSDF 1.1 · OWASP

A checkpoint asks whether there is enough evidence to proceed. For a release, that might mean confirming that critical fixes were retested and recording unresolved risks. The appropriate evidence depends on the product and the harm a failure could cause.

03 / Software security

STRIDE questions

Microsoft’s STRIDE categories help a team notice different kinds of failure. They do not calculate likelihood, rank business risk, or supply protections by themselves. The security concerns below describe the goal being challenged.

Sources: Microsoft overview · Microsoft STRIDE

Six prompts for a design discussion
CategoryPlain-language meaningSecurity concern
SpoofingPretending to be another user, device, or service.Identity: who is this?
TamperingMaking an unauthorized change to information or software.Integrity: was it improperly changed?
RepudiationDisputing an action when reliable evidence is missing.Accountability: can we trace what happened?
Information disclosureExposing information to someone who should not receive it.Confidentiality: who may see it?
Denial of servicePreventing legitimate use of a service.Availability: can people use it?
Elevation of privilegeGaining permissions that were not granted.Authorization: what may this identity do?

04 / Software security

A checkout example

Imagine a customer sends an order to a checkout service, which stores it in an orders database. Customer-controlled input crosses a trust boundary into the store’s system. A protected network connection does not establish that the requested action is allowed.

Sources: Microsoft overview · OWASP

Original teaching example: questions to investigate
Possible problemDefensive question
Another person’s login session is used.How are sessions protected?
An unapproved price is accepted.Does the service validate prices against trusted data?
An order change cannot be traced.Are useful audit records protected?
A customer sees another customer’s order.Is access checked for each order?
Heavy traffic blocks checkout.How is overload limited and recovery handled?
A customer reaches administrator functions.Are privileged actions separately restricted?

Sources: Microsoft STRIDE · OWASP

These are questions, not findings about a real store. A log entry can help establish what happened, but does not automatically prove a person’s identity or intent.

05 / Software security

The PASTA process

PASTA means Process for Attack Simulation and Threat Analysis. Developed by Tony UcedaVélez and Marco M. Morana, it links technical evidence to organizational goals and consequences. Its seven stages can be summarized as follows.

Sources: PASTA originator

  1. Establish the purpose and the losses that matter.
  2. Set the scope: people, technology, and dependencies.
  3. Map components, information movements, and permission boundaries.
  4. Examine evidence about relevant threats.
  5. Connect threats to weaknesses that could enable harm.
  6. Check plausible attack paths using evidence and authorized testing where appropriate.
  7. Analyze consequences, prioritize responses, and document remaining risk.

Sources: PASTA originator

STRIDE can broaden an initial discussion; PASTA supports deeper investigation and prioritization. They can be combined. Neither removes the need to document assumptions and check that the analysis reflects the actual product.

Sources: OWASP · PASTA originator

06 / Software security

NIST guidance

The US National Institute of Standards and Technology’s SSDF describes practices that fit into an existing development process. It does not prescribe a programming language or these six teaching stages.

Sources: NIST SSDF 1.1

Four SSDF practice groups
GroupPurpose
Prepare the OrganizationProvide responsibilities, requirements, tools, and suitable environments.
Protect the SoftwareProtect code and releases; help recipients check release integrity.
Produce Well-Secured SoftwareApply security requirements to design, coding, review, testing, and configuration.
Respond to VulnerabilitiesFind, assess, address, and learn from weaknesses.

Sources: NIST SSDF 1.1

Version status checked 4 September 2026

At that check, SSDF 1.1 was the final core framework. SSDF 1.2, released on 17 December 2025, remained an initial public draft. Its comment period had closed. This is a dated status statement, not a new September 8 verification.

Sources: NIST SSDF 1.1 · NIST publication list · SSDF 1.2 draft

A useful threat model supports a decision and records why. It may justify a changed design, a new test, better monitoring, or keeping the existing approach. Record the owner and what new evidence would trigger a review.

Sources: OWASP

Words explained

Threat
Something that could cause harm.
Vulnerability
A weakness that could enable harm.
Risk
Possible consequences considered together with their likelihood.
Safeguard
A measure intended to reduce risk.
Dependency
Another component or service a product relies on.
Trust boundary
A point where permissions, ownership, or trust assumptions change.
Authentication
Checking an asserted identity or message origin.
Authorization
Deciding which actions an identity is allowed to take.
Provenance
Information about where software came from.
Residual risk
Risk remaining after safeguards are applied.
